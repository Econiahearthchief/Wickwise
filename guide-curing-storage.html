Curing and storing candles

Your candle is poured, cooled, and looks perfect. It's also not done. Curing — the quiet weeks after pouring when fragrance oil finishes binding with the wax — is the most skipped step in candle making and the cause of more "weak scent" complaints than any other single thing. Here's why it matters, how long each wax needs, and how to store both finished candles and raw materials so nothing goes stale.

Why curing matters

When you stir fragrance oil into melted wax, the two aren't truly combined yet — they're just mixed. Over the following days and weeks, the fragrance molecules continue binding with the wax at a molecular level. A candle burned before that binding finishes has fragrance sitting loose in the wax rather than locked in, and loose fragrance doesn't vaporize evenly in the melt pool. The result: weak, uneven hot throw that has nothing to do with your recipe.

This is also why cooling time and cure time are different things. A candle can be hard to the touch in hours and still need two weeks of cure. "It's set" is not "it's ready." Judging a soy candle's scent at 48 hours is like judging bread dough before it rises — you're testing an unfinished product and blaming the recipe for the process.

Cure times by wax

WaxMinimum cureWhy
Soy1–2 weeksPlant-based wax binds fragrance slowly; a full 2 weeks for an honest scent test
Coconut1–2 weeksSimilar to soy — soft natural wax, slow binding, benefits from the full 2 weeks
Beeswax~2 weeksThe slowest of all — dense wax plus its own natural scent competing with the fragrance
Paraffin24–48 hoursPetroleum wax binds fast; ready dramatically sooner than natural waxes
Blends (e.g. parasoy)5–7 daysFalls between its parents — longer than paraffin, shorter than straight soy

These are minimums for a fair test, not magic numbers where day 14 is perfect and day 13 is garbage. When in doubt, wait longer — over-curing doesn't hurt anything, under-curing ruins your evaluation.

Where and how to cure

Curing candles aren't fragile, but the conditions matter:

  • Cool, dark, room temperature — roughly 60–75°F. Not the garage in summer, not next to a sunny window. Heat during cure can push fragrance back out of the wax (the same seeping you see in overheated storage).
  • Airflow, not airtight. Don't shrink-wrap or seal candles airtight for the first few days — let them breathe while the initial volatiles settle. After 3–4 days, lids or loose covers are fine and keep dust off.
  • Upright and undisturbed. Seems obvious, but a curing shelf that gets bumped is how wicks go crooked.
  • Label everything with the pour date. You will not remember which batch is which in two weeks. Painter's tape and a marker on the bottom of each jar. Future you says thanks.

How to tell when a candle is cured

You can't see curing happen, but you can track it:

  • The cold-throw test. Smell the unlit candle every few days. You'll notice the scent getting stronger and rounder over the first week (soy/coconut) — that's binding in progress. When the cold throw stops improving for several days running, the candle is cured.
  • The calendar. Less romantic but reliable: pour date + the table above = earliest test date. Write it on the label.
  • The comparison burn. If you're impatient, burn one candle at 1 week and keep its twin for 2 weeks, then compare. The difference in hot throw is usually obvious — and once you've seen it, you'll never skip cure again.

Never adjust a recipe based on an uncured test. "Needs more fragrance" at day 3 is often "needs more patience" at day 14.

Storing finished candles

A cured candle keeps well if you store it right — and degrades fast if you don't:

  • Out of light. UV fades dyes and degrades fragrance over weeks. A dark shelf or cabinet beats a sunny display table for anything that won't sell immediately.
  • Cool and stable. Heat is the enemy — it softens wax, pushes fragrance out (sweating), and accelerates scent loss. Room temperature or cooler, away from vents and appliances.
  • Covered. Lids, dust covers, or even just keeping them boxed keeps dust off the surface. Dust in the melt pool causes sputtering and soot on the burn.
  • Upright. Storing on the side risks wicks bending and, in warm conditions, wax shifting.
  • How long do they keep? A properly stored container candle holds its scent well for about a year. Past that, top notes fade first — the candle still burns fine, but the bright opening dulls. Sell or gift within the year; don't warehouse.

Shelf life of your materials

Finished candles aren't the only thing that goes stale. Your raw materials have clocks too:

  • Fragrance oils: 1–2 years. Citrus and light fresh scents degrade fastest (around a year); vanilla, woods, and ambers last longest. Store sealed, cool, and dark — an opened bottle in a hot workshop goes off in months. If an oil smells sharp, thin, or "off" compared to when you bought it, it's turned; don't blend with it.
  • Soy wax: ~2 years. Natural waxes slowly oxidize. Old soy can yellow and develop a stale smell that carries into the candle. Buy what you'll use within a year or so.
  • Coconut wax: 1–2 years. Similar to soy — soft natural wax, keep it cool and sealed.
  • Paraffin: essentially indefinite if kept cool, dark, and dry. Petroleum wax doesn't oxidize the way plant waxes do.
  • Beeswax: years. Archaeologists have found usable beeswax in ancient tombs — your block will outlast your candle-making career if kept dry.
  • Wicks: don't really expire, but keep them dry and dust-free. Damp cotton wicks burn poorly.

Write the purchase date on every fragrance bottle and wax case when it arrives. The two seconds it takes saves you from blending with a dead oil six months later and wondering why the batch is flat.

A quick curing reference

Tape this to your curing shelf:

  • Soy / coconut / beeswax: pour → cool undisturbed 24h → move to cool dark shelf → earliest test at 2 weeks
  • Blends: pour → cool undisturbed 24h → earliest test at 5–7 days
  • Paraffin: pour → cool undisturbed → earliest test at 48 hours
  • Melts (any wax): pour → rest 3–5 days → judge
  • Always: label with pour date, keep out of light and heat, don't judge early

Do wax melts need curing?

Yes, but less critically. Melts have no wick and no flame — the scent releases from a warmer's heat, not a melt pool — so the binding matters less for performance. Still, a few days of rest improves scent payoff noticeably, especially for soy melts. Give melts 3–5 days before judging them; the full two weeks isn't necessary the way it is for wicked candles.

The patient maker's advantage

Curing is the cheapest quality upgrade in candle making — it costs nothing but time and shelf space. The makers whose candles throw beautifully aren't using secret oils; they're just not lighting them early. Pour, label, wait, and let chemistry do the work.
