Candle making FAQ

How much fragrance oil do I actually use?

It depends on your wax, not a flat 6–10% rule. Soy and coconut typically max around 10%, paraffin can take up to 12%, and beeswax should generally stay under 6% since it already carries its own natural scent and binds fragrance less readily. Beyond that, your specific fragrance oil's IFRA usage limit can cap you lower than your wax's max — always check the supplier's data sheet, since two "vanilla" oils from different brands can have very different safe limits.

Why did my candle seep oil or smell weak even under the max load?

Seeping usually means the fragrance load was too high for that specific oil's density, not just over a generic wax percentage — heavier oils (like vanillas and some woods) seep at lower percentages than light florals or citrus. Weak throw with normal load is more often a wick or cure problem: an undersized wick won't create enough melt pool to release scent, and most soy/coconut candles need a full 1–2 weeks of cure before they throw properly — testing at 48 hours will always underrate a candle's real performance.

What's the actual difference between CD, ECO, and HTP wicks?

These are different wick families, not interchangeable sizes of the same thing. CD (cored, braided) wicks are the most common all-purpose choice for soy and blends. ECO wicks are flatter and tend to run a bit cooler, often preferred for 100% soy for a cleaner burn. HTP (high-throw-power) wicks are built for stronger hot throw and are common in paraffin and heavily-fragranced candles. A "CD-16" and an "ECO-16" are not the same wick — always match the family to what your wax type and supplier recommend, not just the number.

What is flash point, and why does it matter for pour temperature?

Flash point is the temperature at which a fragrance oil's vapors can ignite — and it's also roughly the temperature above which the scent starts evaporating out of your wax before it ever sets. Adding fragrance above a wax's recommended add-temperature range doesn't just risk safety, it quietly burns off scent you paid for, which is a common hidden cause of "why is my hot throw so weak" that has nothing to do with your fragrance load percentage.

Cold throw vs. hot throw — what's actually going on?

Cold throw is what you smell from the unlit wax at room temperature; hot throw is what fills the room while it's burning. They rely on different mechanisms — cold throw is mostly the fragrance oil sitting near the wax's surface, while hot throw depends on your wick creating a full, hot melt pool that vaporizes fragrance molecules into the air. A candle can have strong cold throw and weak hot throw if the wick is undersized for the jar, even with a correct fragrance load.

How long should I actually cure candles before judging them?

Soy: minimum 48 hours, but 1–2 weeks for a fair scent-throw test. Coconut: similar to soy, often benefiting from the full 2 weeks. Paraffin: cures fastest, usually fine at 24–48 hours. Beeswax: slowest, often wants close to 2 weeks. Judging a candle's throw before its wax type's real cure window is the single most common reason people think a fragrance oil is "weak" when it just hasn't finished binding yet.
